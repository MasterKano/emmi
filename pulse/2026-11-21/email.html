WEEKEND PULSE
The AI trade breaks: worst week for US stocks since April 2025
Week to Fri 20 Nov 2026 · Fri 13 Nov close → Fri 20 Nov close · 5-minute read, then reference lists.

Nvidia's beat, Anthropic's listing and an SEC probe end in a rout

Three blows in four days. Nvidia beat on Tuesday and the relief lasted a day. Anthropic's record $81bn listing on Thursday pulled money out of the rest of the sector, AMD fell on a short-seller report about its China links, and on Friday Reuters reported that the SEC is examining Nvidia's accounting. The S&P 500 fell seven days running, its longest losing streak since February 2020; it and the Dow closed in correction, the Nasdaq turned negative for 2026 and Taiwan had its worst week since 1990.
Rates first hurt, then cushioned. The Fed minutes and Governor Waller's call for a bigger move to “shock people” put a December rise almost fully in the price. Then Friday's rout sent money into Treasuries, and the 10-year had its biggest weekly fall since April 2025.
No shelter in metals. Gold and silver hit 14-month lows as investors sold winners to cover losses; on Friday only consumer staples rose.
What to watch: The main risk is a Fed still hiking into falling markets; any softer language would be the first relief, and credit is the tripwire: junk spreads through the watch line would turn a valuation reset into a funding problem. Watch whether dip-buyers return in thin Thanksgiving trade, as after April 2025; Nvidia now trades on the SEC probe and its leadership, not another earnings beat, and oil trades the surplus unless Gulf export terminals are damaged.

Weekend read

Four longer reads from the week, built from John Authers’ Points of Return, broker research and our own read of the data. Quotes are verbatim and linked; where the sources disagree, we say so and give our view.
After Huang: a crowded trade unwinding, or a thesis breaking?
The bear case for AI this week came with a historical template. Authers opened the week with it, before Nvidia had even reported:
“In every market boom, an indispensable dominant player is eclipsed by the very transformation it wrought. That happened to names like Netscape and AOL as the internet took off; now, the market reaction to Jensen Huang’s resignation in scandalous circumstances suggests a fear that Nvidia Corp., of all companies, might suffer that inglorious distinction for AI.”
— John Authers, Points of Return, Bloomberg Opinion, 16 Nov
The results did little to settle that fear, and the reason matters. Revenue, guidance and margins all beat, yet the reception was lukewarm because the market is no longer pricing the quarter. It is pricing a company with no named chief executive, a board that has ruled out the founder’s return, and inquiries by Congress and the Justice Department into the China allegations. Richard Abbey, writing in Authers’ column, found the sharpest line on it:
“They are Jensen’s numbers from three months ago or two months ago. What [investors] care about is how behavior [changes] going forward.”
— Luke Lango, Innovation Investor, quoted in Points of Return, Bloomberg Opinion, 18 Nov
The money had started moving before Friday. Bank of America’s November fund manager survey, taken in the days the scandal broke, showed the biggest shift away from tech stocks in two decades, a record share of managers saying companies are overinvesting, and a swing back from “no landing” to a soft landing. Managers now expect non-US equities to do better next year, which would end the long run of American exceptionalism. Authers’ own conclusion was measured:
“Fund managers think that tech has a comeuppance that will mean blah US equity performance and an economic soft landing. But not a crisis. It sounds like a decent base case.”
— John Authers, Points of Return, Bloomberg Opinion, 18 Nov
He offers 2006 as the precedent: US tech lagged the market by 16% in the second quarter of that year, then beat it by 26% until the financial crisis. The difference this time is the funding. Abbey’s “AIndicators” instalment showed that six technology groups now carry 9.7% of the spread risk in the US investment-grade bond index, against 7% for the six largest banks (Barclays’ Dominique Toublan), and that the default risk priced into their credit insurance has more than doubled in months, with Oracle and SpaceX at particularly high levels. The column’s most striking line was about the Treasury market:
“It looks ever more as though Big Tech is “crowding out” the government and forcing the Treasury to offer higher rates — the opposite of what typically occurs.”
— Richard Abbey, Points of Return, Bloomberg Opinion, 16 Nov
Then there is the supply of new paper. Anthropic’s raise, with its private placement, was the biggest IPO on record, and OpenAI is expected next year. Nathan Sonnenberg of Pitcairn put the mechanism plainly:
“The markets, while they are large, can only absorb so much capital. If we, the investment bankers, come to market with deals that are too large, that means other securities need to be sold.”
— Nathan Sonnenberg, Pitcairn, quoted in Points of Return, Bloomberg Opinion, 19 Nov
Our read. The evidence fits the de-crowding of a consensus trade with a governance shock on top, not a collapse in AI demand: OpenRouter data cited in Monday’s column show token use still rising, even as the price per token falls. Where we part company with the comfortable base case is the balance sheet. When a handful of borrowers dominate investment-grade spread risk and compete with the Treasury for long money, a hawkish Fed hits the AI build-out through its cost of capital, not its order book. That is the channel to watch over the winter.
The dollar is saying something the Fed is not
The Fed minutes held no surprise on direction: almost all officials expect further rises, and a couple were open to more than a quarter-point if inflation data disappoint. Governor Chris Waller has since made that case in public. The detail worth keeping is how the committee now thinks about AI:
“Many participants viewed the AI buildout as a material source of inflation risk.”
— FOMC minutes, as summarised in Points of Return, Bloomberg Opinion, 19 Nov
In the same minutes, “a number of participants” noted that tougher scrutiny of the sector’s returns and financing could slow AI spending. Read together, the Fed sees the build-out as a source of inflation and a cooling of it as one of the ways tightening works. This week’s equity pain is not a reason for the Fed to stop; it is part of the transmission.
The puzzle is the currency. Implied rate expectations did nothing on Wednesday, yet the dollar surged to its highest in a year and a half. Robin Brooks of the Brookings Institution drew the uncomfortable conclusion:
“It’s clear that the sharp rise in the dollar has totally decoupled from the rate differential, so dollar strength is NOT about the hawkish Fed. That can be a sign that something is else is going on, i.e. something is breaking.”
— Robin Brooks, Brookings Institution, quoted in Points of Return, Bloomberg Opinion, 19 Nov
Gavekal’s Louis-Vincent Gave offered the opposite reading a day earlier. If the public fall of Nvidia’s founder shows that the rule of law applies even at the top, the dollar should strengthen and yields fall; if it shows unpredictability, the reverse. On that test, Authers noted, “the US is looking good so far.”
Our read. Brooks has the better of it. A currency that rises while rate differentials stand still, at the same time as precious metals are sold rather than bought, is behaving as the liquidity asset of last resort. That is a stress signal, and the first casualties are outside the US. Bank Indonesia surprised with a hike to defend the rupiah, JPMorgan’s emerging-market currency gauge sits at its lowest since the Liberation Day tariff sell-off, and Capital Economics’ Jason Tuvey warned that “Credibility is much easier to lose than it is to rebuild.” A dollar that keeps climbing without help from rates would be the clearest sign that the AI unwind has become a funding story.
Hormuz: the screen says calm, the tankers do not
Crude’s indifference to attacks in the Gulf rests on a market that is back in balance. DNB Carnegie’s Helge André Martinsen tracks global inventories rising at a normal seasonal pace and physical premiums over futures that have eroded completely over two months. On his numbers, the re-routing through Saudi Arabia’s Yanbu and the UAE’s Fujairah, which together now load about twice their pre-war volumes, has done the work the Strait cannot.
The flow data turned late in the week, though. DNB’s tanker tracking puts oil through Hormuz below 10 mb/d on a seven-day basis for the first time since 11 September, a net disruption of about 6.3 mb/d against pre-war flows. That sits awkwardly with US Energy Secretary Chris Wright’s claim of record flows through the Strait on Wednesday; one of the two will be revised. Liquefied gas is the starker signal: LNG through Hormuz has fallen to about 18% of pre-war levels, from roughly two-thirds in early November, because LNG carriers retreat faster than oil tankers whenever attacks pick up.
That is why European gas and oil are telling different stories. Seb Kennedy at Energy Flux calls TTF a weather vane, and the weather is the risk that is hardest to price. The Met Office says a sudden stratospheric warming has disrupted the polar vortex unusually early, with a 70% or greater chance of a negative North Atlantic Oscillation, which brings colder, drier conditions. Kennedy spelt out the tail:
“A blast of icy Arctic air across the northern hemisphere could leave Europe and Asia bidding hard for a diminishing pool of flexible LNG cargoes if this were to coincide with a Texan freeze-off that cuts feed gas supply to US Gulf Coast liquefaction plants.”
— Seb Kennedy, Energy Flux, 20 Nov
The cushion is thin. In SP Angel’s 19 November note, EU storage was 70% full against a five-year average of 88%, and Germany only 57%. Further out, the picture flips. Qatar is building a portfolio of 20–30 mtpa of mostly US offtake, a hedge against Hormuz that Kennedy notes also gives Doha a say over when US cargoes are cancelled in a glut. DNB counts this year’s LNG sanctions heading for about 52 mtpa if Mozambique’s Rovuma project goes ahead, and expects the wave of new supply to weigh hard on European and Asian gas prices towards the end of the decade.
Our read. Oil is better cushioned than gas because crude can be piped around the Strait and Qatari LNG cannot. For this winter the risk in European gas is skewed upwards, set by Hormuz LNG and the vortex rather than by speculators. The glut is real, but it is a story for the 2030s, not for January.
Where AI meets the grid
The AI sell-off has not reached the order books of the companies that power data centres. Thunder Said Energy tracks book-to-bill ratios at 55 industrial companies; the trailing twelve months to the third quarter averaged 1.32x, a record and well above the decade’s 1.11x. The leaders are telling: gas turbines at 2.23x, HVAC at 1.83x and power electronics at 1.66x. Rob West thinks the third quarter could be the peak, if policy curbs speculative projects or AI inference moves to smaller, more flexible sites. The first crack showed this month when SoftBank wrote down $3.7bn as it rethought some of its 13 GW of turbine reservations, yet Capital Power said other buyers were willing to swap slots for 2028–30.
Speed, not cost, is setting the price of power. West counts more than 10 GW of fuel-cell orders this year, against about 100 MW a year for most of the past decade, 80% of them for data centres. He now puts the cost of solid-oxide fuel-cell power at 12c/kWh, against 8c for a gas combined-cycle plant: which suggests buyers are paying a premium to get power sooner.
Politics is the other constraint. Data-centre proposals were on the ballot in 41 places at the midterms and all but one failed. Alpine Macro’s Noah Ramos does not expect that to dent the more than 30 GW of capacity due in 2027, because moratoriums target new projects, but he sees part of the next wave moving north:
“They will build gigawatts abroad, and they’re already doing that. The areas that are going to do that are colder regions, as it helps minimize cooling requirements. And then also places that have a high energy footprint, like Canada, and even some of the Nordic nations in Europe.”
— Noah Ramos, Alpine Macro, quoted in Points of Return, Bloomberg Opinion, 16 Nov
Our read. For the Nordic power market this is a slow-burning source of demand that the AI share price does not capture: cold air, clean power and grid space are what the next wave needs. The limit is the same as for the hyperscalers themselves. Higher rates raise the hurdle for each new round of building, so the build-out bends with the cost of capital long before it breaks with the share price.
